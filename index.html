India's fourth-largest IT company confesses

The ₹7,136 crore hole: Satyam's cash was never there

The books showed ₹5,361 crore in cash and bank balances. About ₹321 crore was real.

For years Satyam Computer Services looked like the model Indian IT company: about 50,000 employees, more than 300 global clients, listings on the BSE, NSE and NYSE, and awards for corporate governance. On 7 January 2009 its founder and chairman, B. Ramalinga Raju, wrote to the board and SEBI that the accounts had been manipulated for years.

Of ₹5,361 crore in reported cash and bank balances, ₹5,040 crore did not exist. Interest of ₹376 crore was booked but never earned. Debtors were overstated by about ₹490 crore, and about ₹1,230 crore of funds the promoter had arranged personally never appeared as a liability.

The letter came three weeks after the board approved buying Maytas Properties and a stake in Maytas Infra, both owned by the Raju family, for about US$1.6 billion of Satyam's cash. Investors revolted and the deal was cancelled within 12 hours. Raju described his position as "riding a tiger" he could not get off. The share price fell about 78% that day.

The twin gap

Revenue was inflated by ₹588 crore in Q2 FY09, and operating profit by exactly the same ₹588 crore. Every fake rupee of sales landed as pure profit with zero cost. Real revenue needs salaries and delivery; fake invoices need none.

~₹429 cr

Lazy cash

₹5,361 crore in deposits should earn about ₹429 crore a year at an assumed 8% rate. Satyam earned little interest, and booked ₹376 crore as "accrued" instead of received. Interest that never turns into cash means the deposit may not exist.

8% is our illustrative assumption, for scale only.

Why borrow?

Rich on paper, borrowing in reality

A company sitting on thousands of crores does not take loans. Satyam did, and kept paying dividends and raising capital. Behind it: about ₹1,230 crore the promoter arranged personally, kept off the books.

Slide 3 · Who Failed and WhyAccountability
Every layer trusted the layer above it

Five gatekeepers, one blind spot: nobody checked the cash at the source

Perpetrator

Promoter & top management

What failed

Fake invoices, inflated revenue, forged bank statements and confirmations. Promoter funds of ~₹1,230 cr kept off the books.

Why

Pressure to keep growth and margins on track. A falling, pledged stake needed a high share price. One quarter's gap forced a bigger one the next: the "tiger". Outcome: convicted in 2015, 7-year sentences.

Auditor

Price Waterhouse

What failed

Accepted bank balance confirmations that were forged; did not confirm ₹5,361 cr of cash directly with banks.

Why

Years on the same client, evidence routed through management, weak professional scepticism. Outcome: US SEC fine of $6 mn (2011); SEBI 2-year ban and ~₹13 cr disgorgement (2018).

Board

Board & independent directors

What failed

Approved a ~US$1.6 bn deal with promoter-family firms, paid from company cash, then resigned when investors objected.

Why

Deference to a celebrated founder. Governance awards taken as proof of governance. The audit committee never asked why so much cash earned so little.

Regulators

SEBI & the system

What failed

Promoter pledges were not required to be disclosed. The World Bank ban in Sept 2008 did not trigger a probe.

Why

Rules looked at disclosures, not at whether the cash existed. No independent audit regulator at the time.

Market

Analysts & institutions

What failed

Bought the margin and growth story; ignored lazy cash, borrowing and pledges.

Why

A market favourite with a strong brand. Nobody wanted to be the one who questioned it.

Raju's motive in one move: the Maytas deal would have swapped fictitious cash for real assets. His letter called it the last attempt to fill the fake with the real.

India Inc.
"India's Enron"

Foreign investors questioned Indian governance just months after the 2008 global crisis.

What changed because of Satyam

2009: SEBI makes disclosure of pledged promoter shares mandatory.

Companies Act 2013: mandatory auditor rotation, a code for independent directors, class-action suits, statutory powers for SFIO.

2018: NFRA set up as an independent regulator of auditors.

Government playbook: board takeover and auction, a template later reused for IL&FS.

The Satyam test for any investor

Five questions. Two or more "yes" answers means dig deeper.

  1. Does reported cash earn far less interest than a fixed deposit would?
  2. Is the company borrowing while holding large cash?
  3. Are margins well above peers with no clear reason?
  4. Is the promoter's stake falling or pledged?
  5. Is there a large deal with the promoter's own companies?

Satyam in 2008: five out of five.

Looking ahead: where the next Satyam hides

The same pattern shows up wherever a dominant promoter controls the evidence: cash parked in hard-to-verify places, related-party flows, and pledged stakes in a falling market. Forward-looking checks should watch cash held abroad or with group entities, sudden spikes in "other receivables", auditor resignations mid-term, and pledge levels rising as the share price falls.

Slide 6 · Key Lessons and ConclusionClosing Page
The verdict

When the numbers look perfect, ask for the bank statement

  1. Cash is the one number you can verify. Verify it at the source, not through the people who report it.
  2. Margins far above peers need an explanation, not applause.
  3. Governance awards are not governance. Satyam held them until the day it confessed.
  4. Related-party deals are often a fraud's exit door. Maytas was the attempt to make fake cash real.
  5. Small lies compound. Each inflated quarter forced a bigger lie the next.
  6. Gatekeepers fail together. Auditor, board and market each assumed someone else had checked.

Satyam did not collapse because its business was bad. It collapsed because its numbers were false and every check trusted the one before it.

The real company, with about ₹2,112 crore of quarterly revenue, survived the scandal and lives on inside Tech Mahindra. What died was the trust in its accounts. The auditor rotation, NFRA, pledge disclosure and class-action rules India has today exist largely because of this case.

If the judges ask
Was this a profit fraud or a cash fraud?
Both. Profit was inflated each quarter (₹588 cr in Q2 FY09 alone) and the balance sheet absorbed it as fake cash, interest and debtors.
Why didn't the cash-flow statement expose it?
The cash itself was forged, so profit and cash still reconciled. Only direct bank confirmation would have caught it.
Why the Maytas deal?
Paying "cash" for real family assets would have replaced the fake balance with real property and closed the hole.
Could it happen today?
It is harder (rotation, NFRA, pledge disclosure, electronic confirmations) but promoter-dominated boards remain the weak point.
